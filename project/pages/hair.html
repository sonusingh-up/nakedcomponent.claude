"Sulphate-free" isn't a verdict.

Matching the surfactant to the hair, not the label — and three picks across shampoo, oil, and styling that earned the spot for reasons beyond the marketing checkbox.

By the Naked Compound Research Team· 5 min read· Updated September 2026

"Sulphate-free," "paraben-free," and "for all hair types" have become checkbox marketing rather than formulation information. None of the three tells you whether a product actually suits your hair — sulphate-free surfactant systems are milder and lower-lather, genuinely better for colour-treated hair, but they clean less effectively for a naturally oily scalp than a standard sulphate system would.

We read hair care and styling listings for what the surfactant system, heat-tool plate material, or oil's actual ingredient list tell you — not what the front-of-pack claims imply. Three picks, and the checks worth running on anything else.

Do It Yourself

How to check any listing.

Surfactant check

Match, don't default

Sulphate-free for colour-treated or sensitised hair; standard sulphate for a naturally oily scalp that needs stronger cleansing.

"All hair types" check

A red flag, not a promise

Products genuinely suited to a specific hair type will usually name it. Universal claims are usually marketing, not formulation.

Heat-tool check

Plate material over peak temp

Ceramic or tourmaline plates distribute heat more evenly than bare metal — better for hair health than chasing the highest stated maximum.

Silicone check

Build-up over time

Heavy silicones smooth hair short-term but can build up with repeated use — a clarifying wash every few weeks helps if you notice dullness.

Frequently asked
Is sulphate-free shampoo actually better?
Not universally. Sulphate-free surfactant systems are milder and lower-lather, which suits coloured or chemically treated hair well. Standard sulphate systems clean more effectively for oily scalps. Neither is objectively better — the right choice depends on hair type and scalp condition, not the label alone.
What does "for all hair types" actually mean on a listing?
Usually very little. It's a marketing phrase intended to widen the buyer pool, not a formulation claim. Products genuinely suited to specific hair types (curly, colour-treated, fine, oily-scalp) will usually say so specifically rather than claiming universality.
Are heat-styling temperature claims on straighteners and curlers standardised?
No formal Indian standard governs the accuracy of a stated maximum temperature on hair tools. Ceramic or tourmaline plates tend to distribute heat more evenly than bare metal, which matters more for hair health than the peak number alone.
Does hair oil regrow hair or just condition it?
Most hair oils sold as cosmetics in India are conditioning products — regulated under CDSCO's cosmetic framework, not as a drug with clinically proven regrowth claims. "Reduces hairfall" and "promotes growth" are common marketing phrases; treat them as conditioning benefits unless a product is specifically registered and marketed as a drug.

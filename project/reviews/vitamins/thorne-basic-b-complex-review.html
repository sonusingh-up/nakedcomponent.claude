Mechanism

Why the form of a B vitamin is the entire story

The eight B vitamins are coenzymes — the working parts your cells clip onto enzymes to run energy metabolism, red-blood-cell production, and the methylation reactions that build DNA and neurotransmitters. Most cheap supplements supply them as inexpensive precursor forms (folic acid, cyanocobalamin, pyridoxine) that the body must convert into the active coenzyme forms it actually uses. For most people, that conversion works fine. For a substantial minority, it does not work well — and that is the gap Thorne Basic B Complex is built to close.

The headline example is folate. Synthetic folic acid must be converted, via the enzyme MTHFR (methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase), into L-5-methyltetrahydrofolate — the form that circulates and crosses into cells. A common genetic variant (the MTHFR C677T polymorphism) reduces that enzyme's efficiency, so folic-acid takers with the variant convert it less well and can accumulate unmetabolised folic acid. Supplementing the finished product — L-5-MTHF (methylfolate) — bypasses the bottleneck entirely.1 Thorne uses L-5-MTHF, not folic acid, which is the single most meaningful formulation choice in the product.

The same logic runs through the rest: methylcobalamin is the active, tissue-ready form of B12 (versus cyanocobalamin, which must be de-cyanated); pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (P5P) is the active form of B6 that enzymes use directly (versus pyridoxine); and riboflavin 5'-phosphate (R5P) is active B2. For the full receptor-and-pathway detail on the individual vitamins, see our ingredient deep-dives on methylcobalamin (B12) and the wider B-vitamin family.

🔬

The honest one-liner on "active forms"

Active-form B vitamins are a genuine upgrade for people who convert the synthetic forms poorly (MTHFR variants, some older adults, some GI conditions) and a marginal one for everyone else. They are not a different or more powerful vitamin — they are the finished coenzyme delivered pre-made. Worth paying for if the conversion matters to you; over-spec if you just want to cover a dietary gap and metabolise folic acid normally.

The biotin lab-test caveat — genuinely important

Biotin (B7) in supplements can interfere with certain immunoassay blood tests — notably some thyroid (TSH, T4) and troponin (heart-attack) assays — producing falsely high or low results. This is not unique to Thorne; it applies to any B-complex or biotin supplement. If you have blood work scheduled, tell your doctor you take a B-complex, and it is standard practice to stop biotin-containing supplements for 48–72 hours before testing. This matters far more than any brand difference.

Key concern

Do you actually need the active forms — and the India value question

1. Who genuinely benefits from active-form B vitamins?

This is the honest heart of the review. The active forms are a real advantage for a specific group: people with MTHFR polymorphisms who convert folic acid inefficiently (common enough that it is worth taking seriously), some older adults and people with gastrointestinal conditions whose conversion or absorption is impaired, and anyone who simply prefers to supplement the coenzyme forms directly. For those people, methylfolate and methylcobalamin are a meaningful upgrade.

For everyone else — the majority with normal folate metabolism — synthetic folic acid and cyanocobalamin are absorbed and used perfectly well, and a cheap synthetic B-complex covers a dietary gap for a fraction of the price. So the question is not "is Thorne good?" (it is) but "do I need what makes it expensive?" If you don't know your MTHFR status and have no specific reason to prefer active forms, the pragmatic move is a verified, adequately-dosed B-complex — active-form if affordable, synthetic if not — rather than paying an import premium for a benefit you may not need.

Evidence

What the B vitamins actually do — and the India-relevant deficiencies

The B vitamins' value is overwhelmingly about correcting deficiency, not about topping up someone already replete. Where they matter most in the Indian context:

B12 (methylcobalamin). Vitamin B12 is essentially absent from plant foods, so deficiency is widespread among Indian vegetarians and vegans2 and shows up as fatigue, brain fog, poor concentration, and — untreated — anaemia and irreversible neurological damage. Correcting a genuine B12 deficiency is one of the highest-value things a supplement can do, and methylcobalamin is a well-absorbed, tissue-ready way to do it. This single vitamin is the strongest reason a vegetarian Indian household should own a B-complex like this.

Folate (L-5-MTHF). Folate and B12 work together to keep homocysteine — a marker linked to cardiovascular and cognitive risk — in check; B-vitamin supplementation lowers homocysteine and, in at-risk older adults, has been associated with slowed brain atrophy.3 Methylfolate delivers the active form directly, which matters for the sizeable share of people with reduced MTHFR activity.1 For women of reproductive age, adequate folate status before and during early pregnancy is critical for neural-tube-defect prevention (though pregnancy dosing should be guided by an obstetrician, not a general B-complex).

The broader B-complex. A comprehensive review of B vitamins and brain function concluded that the whole family works interdependently in energy metabolism and neurochemistry, and that supplementation is most beneficial where intake or status is inadequate.4 The honest caveat: if you are already replete, more B vitamins do not make a well-nourished person sharper — the water-soluble excess is simply excreted (the harmless bright-yellow urine from riboflavin is the visible proof).

⚠

The one real toxicity to know: high-dose B6

Unlike most B vitamins, vitamin B6 has a genuine upper limit. Chronic intake of very high-dose B6 (typically well above 100 mg/day for extended periods) can cause sensory peripheral neuropathy — tingling and numbness in the hands and feet — which is usually reversible on stopping but occasionally not.5 Thorne Basic B Complex is a balanced everyday complex, not a megadose product, so this is not a specific concern here — but it is the reason not to stack multiple high-B6 supplements, and a reason "more is better" is the wrong instinct with B vitamins.

🔀

Buy the right one: Basic B vs Stress B-Complex

Thorne makes two B-complexes and it's easy to grab the wrong one. Basic B Complex (this product) provides balanced, everyday doses of all eight Bs in active forms — the correct default for daily use, deficiency prevention, and supporting a vegetarian diet. Stress B-Complex uses higher doses (notably more pantothenic acid) for people under sustained physical or psychological stress. For almost everyone reaching for "a B-complex," Basic B is the one you want. Higher dose is not better here — it's just aimed at a different, narrower use case.

Decision

Buy / don't buy

Buy if

  • You are vegetarian or vegan and want a proven, fully-active, well-tested B-complex — the methylcobalamin alone justifies owning one
  • You have a known MTHFR variant, or simply prefer the coenzyme forms (methylfolate, methyl-B12) delivered directly
  • You value Thorne's testing pedigree and a formulation you don't have to second-guess, and budget is not the primary constraint
  • You already source supplements from iHerb, so the import step is not an obstacle
  • You want the balanced daily complex (Basic B), not a high-dose stress formula

Consider an alternative if

  • Value matters — a domestic methylated B-complex (HK Vitals, TrueBasics) delivers the same active forms at roughly a quarter of the daily cost
  • You metabolise folic acid normally and just want dietary insurance — a cheap synthetic B-complex is adequate for you
  • You only need B12 (a common vegetarian scenario) — a dedicated methylcobalamin tablet is cheaper and sufficient
  • You wanted the higher-dose stress formula — that's Thorne's Stress B-Complex, a different product
  • You are pregnant or planning pregnancy — use a prenatal dosed and chosen with your obstetrician, not a general B-complex
📦

How to use it

Take 1 capsule daily, ideally with food and earlier in the day (B vitamins are mildly energising for some people and can affect sleep if taken late). Sixty capsules is a two-month supply. Expect bright-yellow urine from the riboflavin — harmless and expected, not a sign of waste. Stop biotin-containing supplements 48–72 hours before any blood test and tell your doctor you take a B-complex. Store cool and dry, away from light.

What the brand should fix

Two things Thorne should do for the Indian market

1

Establish an authorised Amazon India channel

This is the most India-relevant product in Thorne's core range — a vegetarian, active-form B-complex aimed squarely at a market with widespread B12 deficiency — yet it is available only through iHerb imports and third-party sellers, with no easy recourse and an inflated price. An authorised Indian importer and a visible India product page would let Thorne compete for a market that currently defaults to domestic brands by necessity, and would narrow the value gap that is this product's only real weakness.

2

Make the Basic-vs-Stress distinction unmissable at the point of sale

Buyers routinely confuse Basic B Complex with Stress B-Complex, and end up with a higher-dose formula they didn't need or a lower-dose one they did. Clearer on-pack and on-listing guidance — "everyday daily foundation" vs "higher-dose for high-stress phases" — would prevent a common, avoidable mismatch, especially for first-time buyers importing sight-unseen.

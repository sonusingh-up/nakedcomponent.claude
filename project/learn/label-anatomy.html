Path 01 · Reading a supplement label

The anatomy of a label — what's mandatory, what's decoration

Turn any jar around and you're looking at two labels printed on top of each other: the one FSSAI and Legal Metrology force the brand to print, and the one the brand's marketing team chose to. Learn to see the seam, and the whole jar gets easier to read.

Lesson 01 of 6 ~7 min Beginner Updated Jul 2026

There are two labels on every jar

The front of a supplement is a billboard. It has one job: get you to pick it up. Big word, bold colour, a promise — "ADVANCED", "LEAN", "PRO". None of that is regulated in any meaningful way, and none of it tells you what's inside.

The back is where the real document lives, and here's the quiet trick of the whole industry: most of the back panel isn't the brand talking to you at all. It's the brand doing the bare minimum the law demands, in the smallest legible font, in the order and format regulators specify. Once you can tell that mandatory layer apart from the decoration wrapped around it, a jar stops being a mood and starts being a spec sheet.

This lesson is just about learning to see the seam. You won't need to memorise a single regulation number — you'll need to recognise a shape.

The anatomy, one panel at a time

Below is a real Indian health-supplement label, flattened into its component rows in the order they physically appear on the jar — hero claims up top, fine print below. The green rows are printed because a rule says they must be. The terracotta rows exist because someone in marketing decided they'd help.

The green layer: what the law makes them print

These are the fields a brand cannot legally leave off a packaged health supplement sold in India. You don't need the statute — you need to know each one exists, so you notice when it's missing (a genuine red flag) or buried (a softer one).

  • The category word. "Health Supplement", "Nutraceutical", or "Food for Special Dietary Use" must appear. It's the legal admission that this is food, not medicine — which is why the advisory row later says so out loud.
  • Ingredient list, descending by weight. The single most information-dense line on the jar. Order is mandated; the previous lesson-of-this-path on proprietary blends is entirely about how brands exploit exactly this rule.
  • Nutritional information. Energy, protein, carbohydrate, fat, and any nutrient the brand makes a claim about, per 100 g and per serving.
  • Veg / non-veg mark. Green filled circle for vegetarian; brown filled triangle for non-veg (the mark was updated in the 2022 amendment). On a whey product this quietly tells you it's dairy-derived.
  • Net quantity, MRP, batch number, manufacture date, and "best before". The Legal Metrology quartet. The next lessons in this path pull each of these apart.
  • FBO name and full address, plus the FSSAI logo and 14-digit licence number. This is who is legally accountable for the jar. No licence number, no legitimate sale.
  • The advisory warnings. "This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease", "Not for medicinal use", "Store in a cool, dry place", "Keep out of reach of children". Mandated by the health-supplement regulations — and often set in the palest 5-point grey on the jar.
ℹ
The tell that never lies

The advisory sentence "not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease" is printed because the law requires it — and it directly contradicts the front-of-jar promise to fix your joints / sleep / immunity. When the front and the fine print disagree, the fine print is the one under oath.

The terracotta layer: what the brand chose

Everything else is design. That isn't automatically dishonest — a clean jar and a confident name are fine. The point is to file it correctly in your head: the hero claim, the invented adjectives ("Ultra", "Isolate-X", "Bioavailable+"), the self-awarded badges ("Lab Tested" with no lab named, "India's No.1" with no source), the athlete photo, the flavour splash. All of it is chosen, none of it is checked, and it should carry exactly as much weight as any other advertisement: some, but not much.

Worked example Reading a jar back-panel in ten seconds
ADVANCED WHEY PRO™ · BUILDS LEAN MUSCLE FAST ★ LAB TESTED · #1 SELLING* · since 2019 Health Supplement Ingredients: Whey Protein Concentrate, Cocoa, Natural & Nature-Identical Flavour, Sucralose (INS 955) Net qty: 1 kg · MRP ₹2,499 (incl. all taxes) Batch: WPC2216 Mfg: 03/2026 Best before: 09/2027 Mktd by: Peak Nutrition Pvt Ltd, Plot 14, MIDC, Pune 411026 FSSAI Lic. No. 10012345000123 Not for medicinal use. Not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Store cool & dry. *based on internal sales data

Top two lines: pure decoration. "Lab Tested" names no lab; the "#1 Selling" asterisk resolves to the brand's own sales data. File under advertising.

Everything from "Health Supplement" down: the mandatory layer, present and correct — category word, ingredients in order, the Legal Metrology quartet, an accountable FBO, a real 14-digit licence, and the advisory that politely contradicts the top line. This jar is compliant. Whether it's good is a question the later lessons answer — but it has nothing to hide structurally, and that already puts it ahead of many.

Why the seam is worth seeing

Because attention is the product being sold. The decoration is engineered to spend your attention before you reach the fields that matter. Flip that: when you turn a jar around, skip straight to the green layer. Is the licence number there? Is the ingredient list honest and specific? Does a real company put its address on it? That thirty-second pass filters out more bad products than any amount of front-panel reading ever will — and it makes the rest of this path click into place.

What to remember

A label is a legal minimum wrapped in an advertisement. Read the minimum first.

  • The green layer — category word, ingredients, nutrition, the Legal Metrology quartet, FBO address, FSSAI licence, advisory — is printed because a rule demands it. Its absence is a red flag; its presence is table stakes.
  • The terracotta layer — hero claim, adjectives, self-made badges — is chosen and unchecked. Weigh it like any advertisement.
  • The mandatory advisory ("not intended to diagnose, treat, cure…") is the label under oath, and it usually contradicts the front. Trust the fine print.
Try it · ~1 min

Do the ten-second back-panel pass on a jar you own.

  1. Find the FSSAI logo and 14-digit licence number. No number, no legitimate sale — stop here if it's missing.
  2. Find the FBO name and full address. Is it a real, findable company, or just a PO box and a first name?
  3. Read the advisory line, then re-read the front claim. Notice them disagreeing. That gap is the whole game.

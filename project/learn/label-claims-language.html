Path 01 · Reading a supplement label

Claims language — “supports”, “promotes”, “clinically dosed”

A supplement is legally food, not medicine — so it may not claim to treat or cure anything. That single rule invented an entire dialect: "supports", "helps maintain", "promotes", "clinically dosed". Once you can hear the weasel in the verb, the claims almost read themselves.

Lesson 05 of 6 ~8 min Beginner Updated Jul 2026

The line a supplement may not cross

Indian law draws a hard boundary. A drug can claim to treat, prevent or cure disease — after clinical trials and regulatory approval. A health supplement is a category of food, and under the FSSAI regulations it explicitly cannot claim to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. (That's why the mandatory advisory from Lesson 01 says exactly that, in the fine print.)

So brands that want you to feel a medical promise, without making one, crowd right up against that line. Here's the whole spectrum, from the claims that are outright illegal on a jar to the one kind that's actually checkable:

The weasel verbs, decoded

The "allowed but vague" band runs on a small vocabulary of soft verbs. They're technically structure–function claims: statements about supporting a normal body process, which the law permits without disease-level proof. Learn to translate them:

The claim says…What it legally meansWhat it doesn't mean
“Supports immunity”Contains a nutrient with a role in normal immune function.That this product will stop you getting sick.
“Helps maintain healthy joints”Contains something involved in joint tissue.That it treats or reverses arthritis.
“Promotes energy metabolism”Contains a B-vitamin or similar cofactor.That it will make you feel more energetic.
“May help…”The word “may” carries no obligation at all.Anything. “May” is the safest word on the jar.

The pattern: a vague verb (supports, helps, promotes) + a normal body function (immunity, energy, joints). It's a claim about the nutrient's biology in general, transplanted onto this product so your brain makes the leap the label legally can't.

“Clinically dosed”, “clinically proven”, “research-backed”

This trio deserves its own warning because it borrows the authority of science while promising almost nothing. Parse each carefully:

  • “Contains a clinically studied ingredient” — true of almost any ingredient. Caffeine is clinically studied. It says nothing about the dose in this jar.
  • “Clinically dosed” — implies the studied dose is present. Sometimes true, often unverifiable — and impossible to confirm behind a proprietary blend (Lesson 04). Trust it only when the label shows the actual amount and it matches the studied dose.
  • “Clinically proven” — the strongest and rarest honest version, requiring a trial of the finished product. Nearly always it really means "proven ingredient", not "proven product". Ask: proven — the ingredient, or this jar?
⚠
The asterisk is a confession

A claim followed by a tiny "*" almost always resolves, in the smallest print, to a disclaimer that undoes it: "*based on ingredient studies", "*these statements have not been evaluated…", "*results may vary". The asterisk is the brand quietly admitting the headline overreached. Always find where it lands.

Worked example One immunity gummy, sentence by sentence
IMMUNO-BOOST GUMMIES Front: “Clinically proven immunity support*” “Fights infections & builds resistance” Back: Each gummy provides Vitamin C (40 mg), Zinc (as gluconate, 5 mg), Elderberry ext. *based on published studies of the individual nutrients. Not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.

“Fights infections & builds resistance” is a disease claim — the kind a supplement isn't allowed to make. It survives on the jar only because the mandatory advisory two lines down formally cancels it.

“Clinically proven*” looks like product proof; the asterisk redirects to "studies of the individual nutrients" — i.e. vitamin C and zinc have immune roles, which was never in doubt. The only verifiable, honest claim on the whole jar is the plain "Vitamin C 40 mg, Zinc 5 mg" — and now you can check that against a real recommended dose.

You don't need to police anyone's grammar. Just do one translation each time: strip the soft verb, ignore the borrowed science, and read down to the plainest factual sentence on the jar. That sentence — usually a boring list of amounts — is the only one making a promise it can keep.

What to remember

Strip the soft verb, ignore the borrowed science, read to the plain fact.

  • A supplement can't legally claim to treat or cure. So disease-flavoured promises hide behind soft verbs — “supports”, “helps maintain”, “promotes” — which are claims about a nutrient's biology, not about the product.
  • “Clinically” words borrow authority cheaply. “Studied ingredient” ≠ “this product works”. Trust “clinically dosed” only when the actual amount is shown and matches the studied dose.
  • Follow every asterisk. It usually lands on a disclaimer that cancels the headline. The only claim you can act on is the plain statement of contents.
Try it · ~1 min

Grade the claims on a jar you can see right now.

  1. Find the boldest claim and place it on the ladder: illegal disease claim, needs-approval, allowed-but-vague, or factual?
  2. Underline the verb. If it's “supports/helps/promotes/may”, translate it to what it legally means.
  3. Chase every asterisk to its fine print, and find the single most factual sentence on the label. That's the only one worth acting on.

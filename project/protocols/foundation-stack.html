What this protocol is

Foundation Stack · May 2026

Four compounds with the best evidence-to-cost ratio for a beginner. Not eight. Not twelve. Four.

The Indian supplement market has crossed ₹16,000 crore. The shelf is full. Pre-workouts with 25 ingredients, "mass gainers" that are 70% maltodextrin, ashwagandha from brands that can't name the extract standardisation. Most of it is noise. This protocol is built on a different premise: if a compound has 50+ RCTs, a clinically established dose, and costs under ₹600 per month at effective dose, it belongs in a beginner stack. If it doesn't meet all three criteria, it waits.

The four compounds that survive that filter: whey protein concentrate, creatine monohydrate, Vitamin D3 + K2, and a form-verified multivitamin. Everything else in this protocol is an upgrade path to consider after the foundation is working — at the three-month mark, not week one.

Before you buy anything

Supplements are the last 5% of a training outcome. If your daily protein from food sits below 1.2–1.4g per kg of bodyweight, no whey supplement compensates for that gap. If you're sleeping 5 hours a night, creatine will not recover your performance. The hierarchy matters: training → nutrition → sleep → supplements — in that order. This protocol assumes 3+ training sessions per week and broadly reasonable nutrition. If those aren't in place, save the ₹1,840 per month for food quality instead.

⚠
The blood test worth running first

Before spending money on Vitamin D, check your serum 25-OH-D level. A Vitamin D test at a NABL-accredited lab (Thyrocare, Metropolis, SRL) costs ₹350–700 and takes 24 hours. If you're already above 50 ng/mL, daily D3 is still safe but less urgent. If you're below 20 ng/mL — which applies to 65–75% of urban Indian adults — it is your highest-impact intervention per rupee. For vegetarians, adding a serum B12 + ferritin panel (₹800–1,200) annually is worthwhile given the deficiency rates in plant-heavy Indian diets.

1.Whey protein concentrate

Wp
Whey Protein Concentrate (WPC-80)
Strong evidence · 200+ RCTs
20–25 g protein per serving · 1–2 servings / day

Whey protein concentrate at 80% protein content is the standard-of-evidence protein supplement. At ₹1,200–1,800 per kg for verified Indian brands, it is the most cost-effective way to hit daily protein targets when food alone falls short — which it consistently does in the typical urban Indian diet.

Mechanism

Whey is the liquid fraction separated from casein during cheese coagulation. Spray-drying concentrates it to ~80% protein by weight. The muscle-building mechanism is well-mapped: whey is unusually rich in leucine (~11% of total amino acids), which acts as the primary activator of mTORC1 (mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1) via direct binding to the Sestrin2-GATOR2 complex.1 mTORC1 activation phosphorylates S6K1 and 4E-BP1, initiating ribosomal biogenesis and mRNA translation — the molecular basis of muscle protein synthesis (MPS). Whey's fast gastric emptying (~2 hours) produces a rapid leucine spike that crosses the ~2.5–3g leucine threshold required to maximally trigger MPS per feeding.2 A standard 25g whey serving provides approximately 2.7g of leucine.

Evidence

Morton et al. (2018) meta-analysed 49 RCTs in 1,863 participants: dietary protein supplementation significantly increased lean mass by 0.75 kg (95% CI: 0.37–1.13 kg) and 1-rep max strength by 2.49 kg versus control in resistance-trained adults.3 Effect was larger in individuals with below-adequate baseline protein intake — the category most Indian beginners occupy. ICMR and ISSN both recommend 1.4–2.0g/kg/day for active individuals; a typical dal-rice diet provides approximately 0.7–0.9g/kg/day of protein from food alone.

Whey concentrate vs isolate — the ₹800 per kg question

Whey isolate (WPI-90) contains less lactose (<1%) and more protein per gram than concentrate. The price premium is real. For most beginners, it is unnecessary. The lactose content in a typical WPC-80 serving is 3–5g. Clinical lactose intolerance symptoms typically manifest above 12–15g in individuals without full lactase deficiency. If you've been consuming chai, dahi, and paneer without issues, concentrate is fine. Switch to isolate only if concentrate consistently causes bloating or urgency. Our full guide on isolate vs concentrate →

Vegetarian note: Whey is lacto-vegetarian — a byproduct of cheese manufacturing, no slaughter involved. Strict vegans should use a pea + rice protein blend instead (see the Pure-Veg Stack for detail). Most whey is FSSAI-registered under Food Products Standards regulations as a permitted food ingredient.

2.Creatine monohydrate

Cr
Creatine Monohydrate
Strong evidence · 300+ RCTs
3–5 g per day · Any time · No loading required

The most replicated ergogenic aid in sports nutrition history. Cheap, boring, and effective. If you're a beginner and not on creatine monohydrate, you are leaving measurable results behind at negligible cost.

Mechanism

Creatine is a nitrogenous compound synthesised endogenously from glycine and arginine in the liver, kidney, and pancreas. It enters skeletal muscle via CrT1 (creatine transporter 1), where creatine kinase catalyses the reversible reaction: creatine + ATP ↔ phosphocreatine (PCr) + ADP.4 During maximal effort lasting 3–15 seconds — the duration of a heavy compound set or sprint — the PCr pool is the primary ATP regeneration system. Supplementation raises total muscle creatine stores by 20–40% above baseline, extending PCr-mediated ATP resynthesis. In practice: more reps at a given load, faster recovery between sets, modestly higher maximal power output. A secondary — and growing — body of RCT evidence shows cognitive benefits, particularly in sleep-deprived states and in vegetarian populations where baseline muscle creatine is low.5

Loading versus maintenance

Loading (20g per day in 4 doses for 5–7 days) saturates muscle creatine stores approximately 21 days faster than a steady 3–5g per day dose. Both approaches reach identical saturation levels. Our full creatine loading analysis covers 27 RCTs in detail. For most beginners, the GI risk (dose-dependent loose stools above 5g in single dose), extra cost, and ~3-week time advantage don't justify loading. Start at 3–5g per day and expect meaningful performance changes within 3–4 weeks.

The vegetarian advantage

Creatine is found almost exclusively in skeletal muscle tissue. A meat-eating adult gets 1–2g per day from dietary sources; vegetarians and vegans get essentially zero. The consequence: vegetarian Indians begin with 20–30% lower baseline muscle creatine concentrations than omnivores and respond proportionally more to supplementation in RCTs.6 This is not a minor difference — vegetarians consistently show larger absolute lean mass and strength gains from identical creatine protocols versus omnivores. If you're vegetarian, creatine is arguably the single highest-value compound on this list.

Creapure vs generic creatine

Unlike whey, where multiple Indian brands produce genuinely equivalent product, creatine purity varies meaningfully. Creapure is patented creatine monohydrate from AlzChem (Germany), independently verified at ≥99.9% purity. The concern with generic creatine is dicyandiamide (DCD) and dihydrotriazine (DHT) — synthesis byproducts detected in lower-quality batches. The Creapure premium (~₹200–400 per 300g) provides real quality assurance. If buying generic, demand a visible batch-level COA from a NABL-accredited laboratory before committing.

3.Vitamin D3 + K2

D₃
Cholecalciferol (D3) + Menaquinone-7 (K2 MK-7)
Strong evidence · 500+ studies
1,000–2,000 IU D3 + 45–90 µg K2 MK-7 · daily · with a fat-containing meal

Vitamin D is not a vitamin in the conventional sense — it is a precursor hormone that, once activated, functions as a nuclear receptor transcription factor in virtually every cell in the body. India has a Vitamin D deficiency epidemic that runs counter to every assumption about a sun-rich country.

Mechanism

Cholecalciferol undergoes sequential hydroxylation: in the liver to 25-hydroxycholecalciferol (calcidiol — the serum marker your lab tests measure), then in the kidney to 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol — the active hormone). Calcitriol binds the VDR (Vitamin D receptor), a nuclear receptor expressed in virtually every nucleated cell — skeletal muscle, immune cells, pancreatic beta cells, neurons, and cardiomyocytes.7 The VDR-calcitriol complex acts as a transcription factor for over 1,000 gene targets. In bone: regulates RANKL/OPG signalling for remodelling and increases intestinal calcium absorption via TRPV6 upregulation. In skeletal muscle: VDR activation influences calcium handling and has been associated with proportionally higher type IIa fast-twitch muscle fibre composition — relevant for strength and power.

Why K2 MK-7 matters: D3 substantially increases intestinal calcium absorption. Menaquinone-7 activates two critical carboxylase enzymes — osteocalcin (which incorporates calcium into bone matrix via gamma-carboxylation) and Matrix Gla Protein or MGP (which inhibits calcium deposition in arterial walls). Without adequate K2, D3-stimulated calcium has nowhere targeted to go. This is not a fringe concern — 4 combination RCTs show D3 + K2 MK-7 producing superior bone mineral density outcomes versus D3 alone, with better arterial compliance markers.8 The ₹100 premium for a D3+K2 product over plain D3 is the best incremental spend in this protocol.

India's Vitamin D deficiency paradox

India receives abundant sunlight. India also has some of the highest Vitamin D deficiency rates among countries with equivalent sun exposure. The mechanisms are not mysterious: high skin melanin requires substantially longer UV-B exposure to produce equivalent pre-vitamin D3 quantities; most urban Indians spend 9–10 hours indoors on workdays; traditional clothing in many regions limits sun-exposed skin surface area; and SPF sunscreen adoption has accelerated in urban Bengaluru, Mumbai, and Delhi.9

Multiple urban cohort studies and NFHS-5 (2019–21) data place 65–75% of urban Indian adults below the 20 ng/mL sufficiency threshold. A 600-person Delhi study found 91.2% below 30 ng/mL.10 The widely prescribed 60,000 IU weekly sachet addresses this with a pharmacological megadose — but produces a non-physiological peak-and-trough serum pattern. Daily 1,000–2,000 IU produces steadier serum levels with more consistent tissue saturation. Our full analysis of the 60,000 IU sachet problem →

4.Multivitamin

Mv
Form-Verified Multivitamin
Moderate evidence · India-specific gaps
1 tablet or capsule per day · with breakfast

Multivitamins don't build muscle. They don't improve performance. They cover the micronutrient gaps that a typical Indian diet — high in cereals, moderate in legumes, low in red meat, highly variable in dairy — consistently creates. The ingredient forms matter more than the headline numbers on the label.

What you're actually covering

Vitamin B12 (methylcobalamin preferred): B12 is found almost exclusively in animal products. Clinical surveys consistently show B12 deficiency in 47–70% of vegetarian Indians, a population representing approximately 38–40% of the country.11 B12 is the coenzyme for methionine synthase (DNA synthesis, one-carbon metabolism, myelin maintenance) and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Progressive deficiency causes macrocytic anaemia and irreversible neurological damage. Methylcobalamin — the active neurological form — is preferred over cyanocobalamin, which requires hepatic conversion and has lower CNS uptake.

Zinc (bisglycinate or picolinate): Zinc is rate-limiting for testosterone synthesis in Leydig cells and a structural cofactor in 300+ metalloenzymes including RNA polymerase and Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase. Phytic acid (phytate) in wheat roti, rice, and legumes chelates zinc and significantly reduces its absorption. Urban Indians consuming roti + dal + rice across 2–3 meals per day are at genuine risk of functional zinc deficiency despite apparent dietary adequacy. Zinc oxide — found in most cheap multivitamins — has approximately 4–8% bioavailability versus ~25–30% for bisglycinate chelates. This is the most important form check on any multivitamin label.

Iron (bisglycinate — for women): NFHS-5 places 57% of Indian women of reproductive age as anaemic. Iron bisglycinate provides ~25–30% bioavailability with minimal GI side effects versus ferrous sulfate's higher GI burden. Many Indian multivitamins exclude iron entirely to avoid regulatory category changes — check explicitly whether it's included, and in what form, if you're a premenopausal woman.

Vitamin B6 (P5P preferred): Pyridoxal-5-phosphate is the active coenzyme form, requiring no hepatic conversion. Required for 100+ transaminase reactions and for the synthesis of GABA, serotonin, and dopamine from their amino acid precursors. Pyridoxine HCl (the common synthetic form) works adequately at RDA doses but introduces peripheral neuropathy risk at mega-doses — irrelevant at multivitamin levels, but worth knowing.

✓
The 30-second multivitamin label check

Flip the bottle to the ingredient list. Find B12 — if it says "cyanocobalamin," it's the cheap synthetic form. Find zinc — if it says "zinc oxide," bioavailability is poor. Find B6 — "P5P" or "pyridoxal-5-phosphate" is the active form. These three ingredients tell you more about a multivitamin's formulation quality than any front-of-label claim. The best currently available Indian option (Carbamide Forte) uses methylcobalamin, P5P, and zinc bisglycinate across the board. Most mass-market products use the cheap forms across all three.

Timing & daily schedule

Supplement timing is less important than daily consistency. The most significant timing decision in this protocol is not "post-workout versus morning" — it's "same time every day so compliance doesn't collapse." That said, there are genuine pharmacological reasons for a few of these timing choices:

Foundation Stack · Daily Schedule
Breakfast
Multivitamin (1 tablet) with food. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K require dietary fat for absorption — always take multivitamins with a meal, never fasted. If your multivitamin contains iron, avoid taking it within 1 hour of tea or coffee — tannins inhibit non-haem iron absorption by 60–80%.
Breakfast or any meal with fat
Vitamin D3 + K2 (1 capsule) with a fat-containing meal. D3 is fat-soluble — bioavailability drops significantly without dietary fat present. Breakfast with ghee, butter, whole milk, or a small handful of nuts is ideal. Lunch or dinner works equally well.
Any time of day
Creatine monohydrate (3–5g) dissolved in water or added to your post-workout shake. Post-workout timing may have a marginal pharmacokinetic edge12 but daily consistency matters far more than the timing window. Creatine mixes poorly in cold water — use warm water or a blender bottle with vigorous shaking.
Post-workout or morning
Whey protein (20–25g). Post-workout is convenient and provides leucine during the elevated MPS sensitivity window, but the evidence that meal timing is critical is substantially weaker than the evidence that total daily protein matters. If a whole-food protein meal arrives within 2 hours of training, the shake can be at any convenient time. Two scoops per day is not necessary unless your total daily protein target is otherwise unreachable.

1–2g EPA+DHA per day from a TOTOX-verified fish oil. Strong cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory evidence base. Vegetarians should use algal DHA instead. Do not buy Indian fish oil without checking freshness data first — most brands have never been independently tested for oxidation.

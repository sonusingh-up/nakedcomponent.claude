How it works

Why caffeine does most of the heavy lifting

Strip a pre-workout down to what has been tested in humans over and over, and you are left mostly with caffeine. It is absorbed within 45–60 minutes, crosses the blood-brain barrier, and works by blocking adenosine — the molecule that builds up through the day and tells your brain you are tired. With adenosine locked out of its A1 and A2A receptors, perceived effort drops and the same set simply feels easier. Guest et al., 2021, JISSN RCT Meta

That is not a placebo effect. A large body of trials shows caffeine at 3–6 mg per kilo of bodyweight raises muscular endurance, power output and time-to-exhaustion across trained and untrained people alike. Grgic et al., 2020, JISSN Meta For a 70 kg lifter that is roughly 210–420 mg — which is also why a single scoop of a 300 mg product plus your morning coffee can push you past a sensible daily ceiling.

The knock-on effect matters too: blocking adenosine indirectly lifts dopamine and noradrenaline signalling, which sharpens focus and drive. This is the "locked in" feeling people chase — and it is almost entirely the caffeine, not the eight other names on the panel.

Beta-alanine and citrulline: real, but conditional

Beta-alanine is the tingle. Taken daily, it raises muscle carnosine, which buffers the hydrogen ions that accumulate and burn during hard sets. The payoff shows up specifically in efforts lasting 60 seconds to about 4 minutes — think a brutal set of 20, or a 400 m sprint. Saunders et al., 2017, BJSM Meta The catch: it works by saturation over weeks of daily 3.2–6.4 g dosing, not from a single pre-workout scoop. If a product gives you 1.6 g only on training days, the tingle is real but the benefit is not. Trexler et al., 2015, JISSN RCT

L-citrulline (or citrulline malate) raises plasma arginine and nitric-oxide availability, widening blood vessels — the "pump". Evidence for extra reps and reduced soreness exists but is mixed and dose-dependent, needing roughly 6–8 g of pure citrulline. Trexler et al., 2019, Eur J Nutr RCT Most Indian pre-workouts that list citrulline hand you 1–3 g — enough to print the word on the label, not enough to matter.

Everything else, and the dosing game

Betaine (2.5 g), tyrosine (1–2 g) and taurine (1–2 g) have modest, situational support. The long tail — proprietary "focus matrices", exotic herbs, alphabet-soup nootropics — is largely there to lengthen the ingredient list. The real trick is underdosing: a brand names a researched ingredient, then provides a token amount so the marketing is technically true while the biology never engages. This is why dose transparency, not the number of ingredients, is the single most useful thing to judge a pre-workout on. Jagim et al., 2019, J Int Soc Sports Nutr Label analysis

Label intelligence

How to read a pre-workout label in India

Good signals

mg
Every active ingredient shown in milligrams

A fully disclosed panel — each ingredient with its own weight, no combined "blend" line — is the single most important sign of an honest product. It lets you check citrulline, beta-alanine and caffeine against the clinical doses yourself. Brands that dose properly have no reason to hide the numbers.

Caf
Caffeine stated as an exact number

Look for a clear figure such as "Caffeine anhydrous 200 mg". This is the one dose you must be able to see to use the product safely and stack it against your coffee. Anything vaguer — "energy blend", "caffeine matrix" — means you cannot count your daily total, which is a safety problem, not just a marketing one.

Cl
Clinical doses on the ingredients that matter

Citrulline 6–8 g, beta-alanine 3.2 g, betaine 2.5 g, creatine 3–5 g. When a product hits these, the biology has a chance to work. Round, science-matching numbers are a good sign; oddly small ones (citrulline 1 g) mean the ingredient is there for the label, not for you.

3P
Third-party or Informed Sport testing

Batch testing for banned substances and label accuracy. Essential if you compete in a tested sport, and a strong trust signal for everyone else. Import lines from ON and a few global brands carry it; the India-packed versions may not, so check the specific batch rather than the brand.

Red flags

!
"Proprietary blend", "energy matrix", "focus complex"

The defining trick of this category. Several ingredients are grouped under one total weight, so a "6,500 mg blend" can be mostly cheap caffeine and creatine with a dusting of the citrulline and beta-alanine named on the front. If you cannot see each dose, assume the expensive ingredients are underdosed.

!
DMAA, DMHA, octodrine, higenamine

Amphetamine-like stimulants tied to heart attacks, stroke and deaths. Banned by the FDA and WADA and not permitted under FSSAI rules, but still found in grey-market imports. Also watch for coded names: "1,3-dimethylamylamine", "2-aminoisoheptane", "geranium extract". Put the tub down.

!
Pixie-dusting: real ingredient, token dose

The label proudly lists citrulline, tyrosine, taurine — then gives you 250–500 mg of each, a fraction of what any trial used. It is technically honest and functionally useless. Cross-check every named ingredient against its clinical dose; if it falls short, you are paying for a story.

!
Caffeine over ~350 mg per scoop, or unstated

Mega-dose "hardcore" pre-workouts push 350–400 mg in a single serving, which leaves no room for any other caffeine in your day and raises the risk of jitters, racing heart and poor sleep. Unlabelled caffeine is worse — you are dosing a stimulant blind. Neither is a sign of a better product.

Frequently asked

Pre-workout — common questions answered

How much caffeine in a pre-workout is safe?
The dose that reliably improves performance is 3–6 mg per kg of bodyweight — roughly 150–400 mg for most adults. Grgic et al., 2020, JISSN The widely cited safe daily ceiling for a healthy adult is about 400 mg, counting every source. Nawrot et al., 2003

The catch in India is stacking. Many scoops carry 300 mg on their own, and a couple of cups of coffee or several cups of chai add another 150–200 mg. That combination can push you well past 400 mg, into jitters, racing heart, anxiety and wrecked sleep. Pick a product that states its caffeine as a clear number, keep an eye on your total across the day, and if a label buries caffeine inside a proprietary blend, treat that as a reason not to buy it — you cannot dose a stimulant you cannot see.
Why does pre-workout make me tingle or itch?
That prickling, flushing feeling on your face, neck and hands is beta-alanine paresthesia. Beta-alanine binds transiently to nerve receptors under the skin, producing a harmless tingle that peaks around 15 minutes and fades within half an hour. Trexler et al., 2015, JISSN It is not an allergic reaction and it is not a signal that the product is "working" — it just means beta-alanine is present.

If it bothers you, split the dose across the day or choose a sustained-release form. And remember the important part: beta-alanine only helps by raising muscle carnosine over weeks of daily 3.2–6.4 g dosing. A single training-day scoop with 1.6 g gives you the tingle without the buffering benefit. If muscular endurance is your goal, dose beta-alanine daily and separately.
Do I actually need a pre-workout?
No. The two ingredients with the strongest evidence — caffeine and creatine — are both available on their own for a fraction of a branded scoop. A cup of black coffee (around 95 mg caffeine) taken 45 minutes before training delivers most of the measurable performance edge for a few rupees. Guest et al., 2021, JISSN

A pre-workout earns its place only if you value the convenience of one dosed scoop and the product is fully labelled and clinically dosed. The everything-in-one tubs with a dozen names on the panel are mostly buying you marketing, not biology. If you enjoy the ritual and the flavour, that is a fair reason to use one — just pick a transparent product and do not expect the exotic ingredients to do much.
What is a proprietary blend and why should I avoid it?
A proprietary blend lists several ingredients under a single combined weight — for example "Explosive Energy Matrix 6,500 mg" — without telling you how much of each is inside. It is legal, and brands defend it as protecting their "formula", but the practical effect is to hide underdosing.

A blend can proudly name citrulline and beta-alanine on the front while providing a token amount of each, with cheap caffeine and creatine making up most of that 6,500 mg. You are paying for the impressive-sounding total, not for effective doses. Jagim et al., 2019, JISSN The fix is simple: if you cannot read the milligrams of every active ingredient, assume the expensive ones are underdosed and buy something fully disclosed instead.
Are DMAA and DMHA pre-workouts legal in India?
No. DMAA (1,3-dimethylamylamine) and DMHA (octodrine) are powerful amphetamine-like stimulants that have been linked to dangerously high blood pressure, heart attacks, stroke and several deaths. Cohen et al., 2018 The US FDA has taken enforcement action against them, WADA bans them in sport, and they are not permitted ingredients under India's FSSAI nutraceutical regulations. FSSAI, 2022

Despite that, they still appear in grey-market imports and a handful of "hardcore" tubs sold through third-party listings, sometimes under disguised names like "geranium extract", "2-aminoisoheptane" or "1,3-dimethylbutylamine". There is no performance benefit that justifies the cardiovascular risk. If you see any of these on a label, do not buy it — and be wary of imported products with no FSSAI licence number.
Is the creatine in most pre-workouts enough?
Usually not. Creatine works by saturating your muscles over time — 3–5 g every single day, training or not — and the benefit comes from staying topped up, not from a pre-workout timing effect. Kreider et al., 2017, JISSN

Many pre-workouts contain only 1–3 g of creatine, and you take them on workout days alone, so you never reach or maintain saturation. Creatine monohydrate is also one of the cheapest, most researched supplements available on its own. The sensible approach is to treat any creatine in your pre-workout as a small bonus and take a separate 3–5 g daily dose of plain monohydrate if strength and muscle are your goals.
Can I take pre-workout every day, and does tolerance build?
The performance-relevant tolerance is to caffeine, and yes, it builds — within one to three weeks of daily use the alertness and reduced-effort benefits blunt noticeably. Temple et al., 2017 Cycling off for a week, or dropping to training-days-only, restores sensitivity. Beta-alanine and citrulline do not build tolerance; beta-alanine actually needs daily use to work at all.

Daily use is fine for a healthy adult who keeps total caffeine under about 400 mg and does not have a heart condition or uncontrolled blood pressure. The bigger practical issue is sleep: caffeine has a half-life of roughly 5–6 hours, so an evening scoop can still be in your system at bedtime. Keep your last dose at least 8–10 hours before you sleep, and take stimulant-free days when you train late.

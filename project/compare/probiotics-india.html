How it works

What "probiotic" actually means — and what it doesn't

The working definition, set by an international expert panel, is precise: probiotics are "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host." Hill et al., 2014, Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol Consensus Three words in that sentence do the heavy lifting — live (dead bacteria do not count), adequate amounts (dose matters), and health benefit (it has to be demonstrated, not assumed).

The part the marketing skips is that the benefit belongs to the strain, not the species. Bacteria are named by genus, species, and then a strain code — Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, or Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745. That code identifies the exact cultured lineage that was put through trials. A different L. rhamnosus off a cheaper production line shares the species name but inherits none of the evidence. Sniffen et al., 2018, PLoS One Review This is the single most important thing to understand about the whole category: no strain code, no evidence.

How a strain earns its keep in the gut

A useful probiotic strain does its work through a few overlapping routes. First, competitive exclusion: it competes with pathogenic bacteria for nutrients and for binding sites on the gut wall, and some strains secrete bacteriocins that directly suppress rivals. Second, it feeds the gut. By fermenting fibre, beneficial strains and the resident microbiome produce short-chain fatty acids — especially butyrate, the preferred fuel of the cells lining the colon. Guarner & Malagelada, 2003, Lancet Mechanistic

Third, it shores up the barrier and talks to the immune system. Certain strains tighten the junctions between gut-lining cells, reducing the leak of inflammatory material into the bloodstream, and they nudge immune signalling toward tolerance — promoting regulatory T-cells and secretory IgA rather than a full inflammatory response. Sanders et al., 2019, Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol Mechanistic Crucially, most supplement strains are transient: they pass through and act while present, but do not permanently colonise. That is why a probiotic is a course you keep taking for an effect, not a one-time reseeding of your gut.

The survival problem the front label ignores

To do any of this, the bacteria have to arrive alive. Stomach acid and bile are designed to kill microbes, so a large fraction of a swallowed dose never reaches the colon — which is why strains are tested at specific doses and why delivery matters (acid-resistant strains, delayed-release capsules, or spore-forming and yeast species that shrug off both acid and heat). WGO, 2017 Guideline

Then there is the shelf. Live bacteria die steadily in storage, faster when warm. A label that guarantees CFU "at time of manufacture" is quietly telling you nothing about the count at purchase, let alone at the end of a year on an unrefrigerated Indian shelf. The honest number is CFU "guaranteed through end of shelf life" — and for Indian conditions, heat-stable formats (Bacillus coagulans, Bacillus clausii, Saccharomyces boulardii) sidestep the cold-chain lottery entirely. Fenster et al., 2019, Microorganisms Stability review

Label intelligence

How to read a probiotic label in India

Good signals

GG
Full strain code — genus, species and designation

The gold standard is a complete name such as "Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (ATCC 53103)" or "Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745". That code is the only thing that ties the product to a real trial. If the strain is named in full, the manufacturer is standing behind specific evidence rather than a genus.

EXP
CFU guaranteed through end of shelf life

Look for wording like "10 billion CFU guaranteed until expiry", not "at time of manufacture". This is the count that actually reaches your gut on the last day the product is legal to sell. It is the difference between an honest dose and a number that only ever existed inside the factory.

FIT
Strain matched to your actual goal

Good products pick strains for a job: S. boulardii or LGG for antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, B. longum 35624 for IBS symptoms. A label that explains which strain is for what — rather than a scattergun blend "for overall wellness" — signals a formulator who read the trials.

SPO
Heat-stable format for Indian conditions

Spore-forming Bacillus coagulans and Bacillus clausii, and the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii, survive stomach acid and warm shelves far better than delicate live Lactobacillus. For a product that will sit unrefrigerated in an Indian pharmacy, this is a genuine reliability advantage.

Red flags

!
Species only, no strain code

"Contains Lactobacillus acidophilus and Bifidobacterium bifidum" with no letters or numbers after the species is the category's defining tell. You cannot map it to any study, so every efficacy claim on the box is unmoored from evidence. Treat a code-less blend as an unknown, however impressive the CFU total.

!
CFU counted "at time of manufacture"

This phrasing lets a brand print a big number that decays away in storage. Combined with no refrigeration, the live count at purchase is anyone's guess. If the label will not guarantee CFU through expiry, assume the real dose is materially lower than advertised.

!
"50 / 100 billion CFU, 15 strains" as the headline

Huge CFU counts and long strain lists are marketing, not evidence. Trials used defined strains at defined (often modest) doses; a giant multi-strain number with no per-strain codes or dosing rationale usually signals the opposite of rigour. More strains and more billions is not more science.

!
Vague "immunity / detox / gut cleanse" claims

The evidence base is narrow and specific — mostly diarrhoea prevention and some IBS relief. Broad promises to "boost immunity", "detox the gut" or "flush toxins" are unsupported and a sign the product is sold on vibes. A honest probiotic states a defined use, not a lifestyle.

Frequently asked

Probiotics — common questions answered

Do probiotics actually work?
For specific jobs, with specific strains, yes. The strongest evidence is for preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea — a Cochrane review found Saccharomyces boulardii and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG meaningfully reduce the risk — and for shortening acute infectious diarrhoea in children. Goldenberg et al., 2017, Cochrane Certain strains also help some people with IBS. Ford et al., 2018, Am J Gastroenterol

What is not well supported is the broad "boosts immunity", "detox" or "daily gut health for everyone" marketing on most tubs. A probiotic is a targeted tool for a defined problem, not a general tonic. If you do not have a specific reason to take one — an antibiotic course, a diagnosed gut issue — the evidence that a daily probiotic helps an already-healthy person is thin.
Does the strain really matter, or is any Lactobacillus fine?
The strain is almost the entire story. Benefits are strain-specific, not species-specific: two bacteria can share the species name Lactobacillus rhamnosus yet behave completely differently, because the evidence attaches to one exact cultured lineage identified by a code — GG, or ATCC 53103. Sniffen et al., 2018, PLoS One

In practice this means a tub that says only "Lactobacillus rhamnosus" or "multi-strain blend", with no letters or numbers after the species, cannot be matched to any published trial. You are buying a genus and hoping. A proper label names genus, species and strain designation for each organism — and if it does not, treat every efficacy claim on the box as unproven for that specific product.
What does CFU mean and how much do I need?
CFU — colony-forming units — is the count of live organisms per dose. Clinical trials have used anywhere from about 1 billion to 50 billion CFU depending on the strain and the goal, so bigger is not automatically better; the right dose is whatever the trials for that specific strain used. WGO, 2017

The number that trips people up is timing. Many Indian labels quote CFU "at time of manufacture", but live bacteria die steadily in storage — faster in heat. So a tub boasting "50 billion CFU" made a year ago and stored warm may deliver a small fraction of that to your gut. Look for CFU "guaranteed through end of shelf life"; that is the only count that reflects what you actually swallow.
Do probiotics need refrigeration in India?
Many do, and it is a real problem here. Live Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium products are temperature-sensitive, and India's warm climate plus an unreliable retail cold chain means a lot of product loses viability before it reaches you. Fenster et al., 2019, Microorganisms If something needs refrigeration but sat on a warm pharmacy shelf, assume the live count is well below label.

The way around this is format. Spore-forming Bacillus coagulans and Bacillus clausii, and the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii, are far more heat-stable and survive both storage and stomach acid much better than delicate live cultures. For Indian conditions, choosing a spore or yeast-based product removes the cold-chain gamble almost entirely — which is a big reason they rank well in this guide.
Is curd or dahi as good as a probiotic supplement?
For everyday gut health, home-set dahi is genuinely good — it is live, cheap, and part of a fermented-food-rich diet that supports the microbiome. What it is not is a defined probiotic: the strains in curd are undefined and vary from batch to batch and household to household, so you cannot tie it to a specific clinical outcome.

So the honest answer depends on the goal. For general wellbeing, dahi and other fermented foods (idli, dosa batter, pickles) are excellent and you do not need a supplement at all. For a specific medical job — preventing diarrhoea during an antibiotic course, trialling relief for IBS — you want a named, evidence-backed strain at a known dose, which curd cannot provide. They are complements, not competitors.
Should I take probiotics with antibiotics?
This is one of the few probiotic uses with solid backing. Starting Saccharomyces boulardii or Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG alongside an antibiotic course reduces the risk of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. Goldenberg et al., 2017, Cochrane Szajewska & Kołodziej, 2015 Given how freely antibiotics are used in India, this is a genuinely useful, low-cost habit.

Two practical points. First, S. boulardii is a yeast, so the antibiotic does not kill it — an advantage that makes it a particularly good pairing. Second, if you use a bacterial probiotic, separate it from the antibiotic dose by about two hours so the drug does not wipe it out. Begin the probiotic when you start the antibiotic, and continue for a week or two after finishing. There is a separate, more nuanced debate about probiotics slowing microbiome recovery after antibiotics, but for preventing the diarrhoea itself, the evidence is favourable. Suez et al., 2018, Cell
Are probiotics safe, and can anyone take them?
For healthy people, the well-studied strains have a strong safety record. The usual side effect is mild gas or bloating in the first few days as your gut adjusts, which typically settles. Sanders et al., 2019, Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol

The important exception is people who are seriously ill or immunocompromised — those in intensive care, with central venous catheters, after major surgery, or with severe immune suppression. In these groups, swallowing large numbers of live organisms carries a real, if uncommon, risk of infection, and probiotics should only be used under medical supervision. If you have a significant medical condition or are caring for someone who does, check with a doctor before starting rather than treating a probiotic as automatically harmless.

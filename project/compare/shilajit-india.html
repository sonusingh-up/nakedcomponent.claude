What the research says

The evidence on shilajit

Supported by RCT data
+20%

Testosterone in middle-aged men

Pandit et al. (2016, Journal of Medicinal Association) — 96 healthy men aged 45–55, 250mg purified shilajit extract twice daily for 90 days. Total testosterone rose 20.45%, free testosterone 20.7%. One RCT, small sample. The result is biologically plausible but not replicated at scale. Also: sperm count and motility improved in infertile men (Biswas et al., 2010).

Not supported by the evidence
0

Muscle gain, libido, "every disease"

No RCT shows meaningful muscle mass increases from shilajit alone. Libido improvement is plausible if testosterone was genuinely low, but shilajit is not an aphrodisiac in the Western clinical sense. Traditional Ayurvedic texts make sweeping claims — those are not substitutes for controlled trials. The anti-aging and cognitive claims circulating online have no RCT support in humans.

Label decoder

What separates a clean product from a risky one

The Indian shilajit market has a significant adulteration problem. Lab testing by independent reporters has found lead and arsenic above permissible limits in products from well-known sellers. This is the checklist that matters.

Green signals

✓
Heavy metal COA from a NABL-accredited lab

Lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium — all four must be below Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia limits. If the brand doesn't publish this, walk away. Not a nicety: mandatory for safe use.

✓
Fulvic acid % declared on label

Quality shilajit delivers ≥60% total fulvic + humic acids. If the label just says "500mg shilajit extract" without specifying fulvic acid content, you have no idea what you're buying.

✓
Himalayan or Altai sourcing stated

Shilajit from the Himalayas or Altai mountains has the longest track record. Chinese Altai is acceptable if quality-tested. Vague "pure mountain resin" with no origin detail is a red flag.

✓
Purified extract or resin, not raw powder

Raw shilajit powder is unprocessed rock exudate with inconsistent composition and higher contamination risk. Purified extract or properly processed resin is what was used in the one meaningful RCT.

Red flags

✗
"Shilajit Gold" or multi-herb blends

Dabur, Zandu, and several Ayurvedic brands sell shilajit in combination with ashwagandha, saffron, and gold bhasma. The actual shilajit dose is often under 50–100mg. Don't confuse this with a shilajit supplement.

✗
Price under ₹400 for a month's supply

Genuine purified shilajit extract or resin cannot be sourced, processed, and sold at ₹350 for 60 capsules. Sub-₹400 products are almost certainly adulterated or drastically underdosed with fillers.

✗
Dissolves completely clear in water

Authentic shilajit leaves a slight sediment or coloured residue when dissolved. Crystal-clear dissolution suggests processing that strips the active compounds or outright substitution with a dark-coloured thickener.

✗
Claims of "curing" diseases or sexual "supercharge"

FSSAI-compliant supplements cannot make disease cure claims. Brands that do are either lying to you or operating outside regulatory bounds — neither is reassuring about their manufacturing standards.

Common questions

FAQ

Is resin shilajit better than capsules?
Resin is the traditional form and theoretically closer to the raw material. But the form is almost irrelevant compared to purity. A clean extract capsule from a brand with a NABL-accredited heavy metal report beats an adulterated "pure Himalayan resin" from an unverified seller every single time. The one RCT people cite used a purified, processed extract — not raw resin scraped off a rock.
How much shilajit should I take?
The Pandit et al. (2016) trial used 250mg of purified shilajit extract twice daily — 500mg total across the day, not 500mg at once. Most Indian capsule products dose 500mg once daily, which may be lower in peak exposure than the studied protocol. Splitting a 500mg dose into 250mg morning + 250mg evening is a reasonable approach if you want to match the trial design.
What are the signs of adulterated shilajit?
Four quick checks: (1) dissolves crystal clear in water — authentic shilajit leaves colour and sediment; (2) price under ₹400 for 60 capsules — not economically viable with genuine sourcing; (3) no heavy metal COA published or available on request; (4) "Shilajit Gold" branding with ginseng, saffron, or gold bhasma listed — the actual shilajit content in these blends is usually under 100mg per capsule.
Is shilajit safe for daily use?
Purified, tested shilajit appears safe at 250–500mg/day based on trials up to 90 days. The primary risk in India is buying a product that hasn't been tested for heavy metals. Lead and arsenic are the main concerns — not because shilajit itself is toxic, but because the rock exudate can concentrate whatever minerals are in the surrounding geology. People with iron overload (haemochromatosis) should avoid it, as shilajit can increase iron absorption.

Hormonal health & cycle syncing: a practical guide for modern Indian women.

Your energy, appetite, sleep, skin, and mood all change across the month — not because something is wrong with you, but because your hormones are doing exactly what they are supposed to do. This guide explains what is happening in each phase of your cycle, what your body actually needs, and how to use this information practically. Not as a rigid protocol. As a framework for working with your biology instead of against it.

What cycle syncing actually is — and what it isn't

Let me start with what cycle syncing is not. It is not a rigid protocol that tells you to eat only flaxseeds on Day 3, or that you cannot do strength training during your period, or that your worth as a productive person peaks at ovulation. Most of what circulates on Instagram under the #cyclesyncing tag is wellness content dressed up as biology, and it does more harm than good by making women feel they are failing their cycle.

What cycle syncing is is much simpler: the practice of noticing that your hormones change across the month, and making small, flexible adjustments to nutrition, movement, sleep, and supplementation to support what your body is doing at each stage. Not fighting the fatigue of your menstrual phase with the same expectations you bring to ovulation week. Not eating identically across a four-phase hormonal cycle. Not scheduling your most demanding presentations on Day 26 when progesterone is declining and cortisol sensitivity is highest — unless you have no other choice.

The science supporting cycle syncing as a formal structured intervention is still limited. A 2024 study published in Neuropsychopharmacology (Pritschet et al.) found that brain network connectivity and complexity measurably peak in the late follicular phase — which is consistent with the experiential pattern many women describe of feeling sharper and more motivated in the first half of their cycle. But translating this into a rigid prescription is still beyond what the evidence supports. (observational)

What the evidence does support — strongly, across multiple metabolomics and physiology studies — is that the hormonal shifts of the menstrual cycle produce real, measurable changes in energy metabolism, insulin sensitivity, amino acid turnover, neurotransmitter precursor availability, and sleep architecture. (observational) Acknowledging this is not pseudoscience. Ignoring it is wilful blindness to how the female body works.

The hormones — a non-textbook explanation

You do not need to memorise hormone levels to use this guide. But you do need to understand roughly what the four main players are doing, because the entire framework of cycle syncing rests on their pattern across the month.

Oestrogen (E2)

Rises through the follicular phase, peaks just before ovulation, rises again in the luteal phase, then falls before menstruation. Drives energy, insulin sensitivity, and mood elevation. Acts on serotonin receptors — which is why low oestrogen correlates with low mood.

Progesterone

Produced by the corpus luteum after ovulation. Rises through the luteal phase, falls sharply before menstruation. Has a calming, slightly sedating effect via GABA modulation. Raises body temperature, increases appetite, and is the primary driver of PMS symptoms when it drops.

LH & FSH

Luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone — released by the pituitary. FSH drives follicle development in the early cycle; LH spikes sharply to trigger ovulation (the LH surge). This is what ovulation predictor kits (OPKs) detect.

Testosterone

Women produce testosterone — typically 1/10th of male levels — in the ovaries and adrenal glands. Peaks around ovulation, contributing to libido and assertiveness. Drops in the luteal phase along with oestrogen as PMS sets in.

The four phases — a quick orientation before we go deep

A typical menstrual cycle runs 24–38 days, with 28 days being the textbook average. The four phases do not divide neatly into 7-day blocks in most women — your follicular phase may be longer or shorter depending on when you ovulate, which itself varies cycle to cycle. Stress, illness, travel, and sleep disruption all affect timing. The day numbers below are approximate and based on a 28-day reference cycle.

Phase 1 Menstrual phase Days 1–5 (approximately)

Day 1 is the first day of full menstrual flow — not spotting. Both oestrogen and progesterone are at their lowest. Prostaglandins cause uterine contractions (cramps) as the lining sheds. Energy is typically at its monthly low. This is a legitimate physiological rest signal, not a character flaw.

What your body is doing
  • Uterine lining shedding; prostaglandin-driven contractions
  • Oestrogen and progesterone at monthly nadir
  • Core body temperature drops slightly
  • Serotonin levels reduced (oestrogen drives serotonin synthesis)
  • Iron losses from blood — typically 30–80mL per cycle
Nutrition focus
  • Iron-rich foods — lentils, spinach, sesame, jaggery; pair with vitamin C source
  • Anti-inflammatory foods — turmeric with black pepper, ginger, omega-3 rich foods (walnuts, flaxseed)
  • Magnesium-rich foods — dark chocolate, pumpkin seeds, banana, almonds; magnesium reduces prostaglandin-driven cramping (RCT)
  • Warm, cooked foods — easier on digestion when motility is irregular
  • Reduce caffeine — worsens cramping and disrupts already-poor sleep
Movement & recovery
  • Restorative yoga, slow walking, light stretching — on heavy flow days
  • Listen to actual energy — if it's there, use it; if not, rest
  • Heat on lower abdomen reduces prostaglandin cramping meaningfully (RCT)
  • Avoid cold-exposure exercises on days with severe cramping

After menstruation ends, FSH triggers follicle development in the ovaries, and oestrogen begins its rise. This is typically the "good week" — energy rises, mood improves, insulin sensitivity is at its best. The brain's prefrontal cortex is more active, working memory is sharper, and many women report feeling more creative and socially engaged. A 2024 neuroimaging study (Pritschet et al., Neuropsychopharmacology) confirmed that brain network complexity peaks in the late follicular phase. (observational)

What your body is doing
  • Oestrogen rising — improving serotonin synthesis and mood
  • Insulin sensitivity at its best — cells respond well to glucose
  • Follicle maturing in the ovary; FSH declining as E2 takes over
  • Cervical mucus becoming more fertile-type (clearer, stretchy)
  • Appetite naturally lower — oestrogen suppresses appetite slightly
Nutrition focus
  • Lean proteins — good insulin sensitivity means protein is well-utilised for muscle repair
  • Fermented foods — curd, idli, dosa, kanji; oestrogen metabolism requires a healthy gut microbiome
  • Cruciferous vegetables — broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage; contain indole-3-carbinol which supports healthy oestrogen detoxification via the liver
  • Complex carbohydrates are fine — insulin sensitivity is optimal
  • Lighter meals generally feel natural given reduced appetite
Movement focus
  • Best phase for high-intensity training — energy and recovery are both high
  • Strength training responds well; oestrogen supports muscle protein synthesis
  • HIIT, running, cycling — all appropriate
  • Social exercise feels more motivating during this phase for most women
  • Good phase for learning new physical skills — neuroplasticity is elevated

Oestrogen peaks, triggering the LH surge from the pituitary — which causes the mature follicle to rupture and release an egg. Testosterone also peaks around ovulation, contributing to confidence, assertiveness, and libido. This is the shortest phase but typically the energetically highest. Many women feel at their most socially capable and verbally articulate during this window.

What your body is doing
  • LH surge triggers ovulation — detectable with OPK strips
  • Oestrogen at monthly peak — serotonin and dopamine elevated
  • Testosterone peak — libido and confidence elevated
  • Core temperature rises slightly post-ovulation (BBT tracking)
  • Cervical mucus at peak fertility — egg-white consistency
Nutrition focus
  • Anti-inflammatory foods — the ovulatory process involves a controlled inflammatory response; omega-3s support it
  • Zinc-rich foods — pumpkin seeds, chickpeas; zinc supports follicle maturation and egg quality
  • Fibre for oestrogen clearance — excess oestrogen is cleared via the gut; fibre ensures it doesn't recirculate
  • Light, clean meals — appetite remains moderate
Movement focus
  • Peak performance window for most women — schedule demanding workouts here
  • High-intensity intervals, heavy lifting, competitive sport all appropriate
  • Note: ligament laxity increases around ovulation (oestrogen effect) — warm up thoroughly; ACL injury risk is slightly elevated (observational)
  • Socially energising exercise works well — team sport, group classes

After ovulation, the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum and begins secreting progesterone. Progesterone is calming (via GABA modulation) in the early luteal phase, but as it falls in the late luteal phase — and especially as oestrogen also declines — PMS symptoms emerge. Body temperature is elevated, appetite increases (particularly for carbohydrates, which drive serotonin synthesis), and sleep quality typically worsens. A 2019 metabolomics study in Scientific Reports found that 39 amino acids and derivatives decreased in the luteal phase, indicating an anabolic state driven by elevated progesterone. (observational)

What your body is doing
  • Corpus luteum producing progesterone — peaks around Day 21
  • Progesterone raises metabolic rate by ~2.5–10% — real, measurable increase
  • Insulin sensitivity declines slightly — glucose uptake less efficient
  • Progesterone and oestrogen falling in late luteal — PMS territory
  • Serotonin synthesis decreases as oestrogen falls
  • Amino acid catabolism increases — protein needs are higher
Nutrition focus
  • Increase protein — amino acid catabolism is elevated; 1.6–2g/kg supports this phase better than earlier phases
  • Complex carbohydrates — support serotonin synthesis; sweet potatoes, oats, brown rice help with carb cravings
  • Magnesium — reduces PMS symptoms including cramping, bloating, and mood disruption (RCT)
  • B6 — pyridoxal-5-phosphate supports serotonin and dopamine synthesis; effective for PMS mood symptoms (RCT)
  • Reduce salt — sodium worsens water retention and bloating
  • Reduce alcohol — worsens oestrogen imbalance and sleep disruption
Movement focus
  • Early luteal: maintain intensity — progesterone supports muscle retention
  • Late luteal (Days 24–28): reduce intensity if fatigue or pain is present; this is physiologically appropriate, not weakness
  • Strength training remains beneficial throughout luteal phase
  • Yoga and pilates manage PMS symptoms; good RCT evidence for yoga reducing dysmenorrhea (RCT)
  • Cardio can worsen bloating in late luteal — reduce session length if uncomfortable

Supplements — what the evidence actually supports, phase by phase

This is the section most cycle syncing content gets wrong. The market is full of "phase-specific supplement packs" that contain 12 ingredients per phase, most at sub-clinical doses, at prices that reflect the packaging more than the evidence. I am going to give you what the actual clinical literature says, what doses are supported, and what is available at reasonable prices in India.

⚠

Supplements do not override lifestyle — they support it

The most effective interventions for hormonal health are not supplements. They are: adequate sleep (7–9 hours), stable blood sugar through regular meals, movement appropriate to your phase, and stress management. Supplements address specific deficiencies or provide targeted support — they cannot compensate for chronic sleep deprivation or a diet of ultra-processed food. Always test before you supplement. Get serum ferritin, B12, folate, and vitamin D before adding anything to your routine.

The India-specific layer — because your cycle doesn't exist in a vacuum

Most cycle syncing content is written for women in the West with access to a broad, year-round variety of fresh produce, standard work hours, and no significant cultural pressure around menstruation. The Indian context differs in ways that matter.

Iron deficiency is the default, not the exception. As covered in our iron deficiency guide, 59% of Indian women aged 15–49 are anaemic. Running on depleted iron stores makes every phase of the cycle harder — energy is lower in the follicular phase, cognitive function suffers, and PMS is worse in the luteal phase. Iron is not a menstrual phase supplement. It is a baseline correction for most Indian women who have not tested and corrected their ferritin.

Vitamin D deficiency is structural. Despite living in a subtropical country, most urban Indian women are significantly vitamin D deficient — covered at length in our Thorne D/K2 review and the iron guide. Vitamin D receptors are present in ovarian tissue. Deficiency is associated with cycle irregularity, PCOS severity, and impaired progesterone production. Supplementing D3 is not a luxury — it is a physiological necessity for most Indian women.

Phytate-heavy vegetarian diets suppress zinc and iron absorption. The same plant-based diet that is culturally and ethically appropriate for millions of Indian women is also rich in phytates that inhibit iron and zinc absorption. The cycle syncing nutrition recommendations above — which include zinc-rich seeds and iron-rich lentils — need to be paired with vitamin C and timed away from chai for them to work. (observational)

Menstruation is still stigmatised in many contexts. Resting during your menstrual phase, declining to do strenuous physical work when you are cramping, or discussing your cycle health with a physician are all appropriate behaviours that face unnecessary social friction in many Indian work and household environments. Cycle syncing is not an excuse for avoidance — it is a framework for appropriate self-awareness, and the societal pressure to pretend no hormonal variation exists across the month is itself a health risk.

When to stop self-managing and see a doctor

🚩 Symptoms that require clinical evaluation, not cycle syncing

Cycle syncing is a self-care framework for women with broadly typical cycles. The following symptoms indicate potential underlying conditions — PCOS, endometriosis, thyroid dysfunction, premature ovarian insufficiency, or fibroids — that require diagnosis and treatment, not just lifestyle adjustment.

Cycles shorter than 21 days or longer than 38 days consistently, over more than 3 months
Skipping periods for 3+ consecutive months without pregnancy or known cause
Bleeding so heavy that you soak through a pad or tampon every hour for 2+ consecutive hours
Severe pelvic pain at any point in the cycle, especially mid-cycle or outside menstruation
PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) — PMS so severe it interferes with work, relationships, or daily function; this is a diagnosable condition requiring medical treatment
Significant unexplained hair loss, facial hair growth, or acne across the cycle — potential signs of hyperandrogenaemia or PCOS
Fatigue so profound it doesn't match the phase — especially if present throughout the follicular phase when energy should be rising
Intermenstrual bleeding — any bleeding between periods needs investigation
